How Bright Is The CMB?

The light we see is only a tiny fraction of the electromagnetic spectrum. Use the buttons below the first panel to see the Milky Way in many more frequency bands. The second panel shows the range of the collecting telescope. Under 'Microwave,' you can select a specific Planck frequency band. The second panel overlays the Planck CMB power spectrum on top of the microwave band you've chosen.

All the bands are normalized to their own maximum intensity, similar to the way your eyes adjust to the available light. But the various bands are not all equally bright in the sky. Click Relative Intensity to see how the different bands of the electromagnetic spectrum compare to each other. Some bands will produce a black image; that's not an error. Look at how they compare on the plot. Which bands are the brightest?

About 99.9% of the photons in the Universe are cosmic microwave background radiation. Despite their low energy, they contribute roughly 95% of the electromagnetic radiation in the sky. You could say, to a good approximation, that until we learned to see the microwave sky, we had never seen the Universe.

Why are we not seeing the uniform glow of the CMB in the microwave bands? In these images, the difference between light and dark was stretched to highlight galaxy structure. To see the CMB, let's process the data differently. Click Logarithmic Intensity to see the microwave bands with a compressed dynamic range.

This site uses image, instrument response, bandpass, and calibrated intensity data from the Planck mission (ESA), the Haslam 408 MHz all-sky survey and its Remazeilles et al. reprocessing, WISE and IRAS/IRIS, the Digitized Sky Survey (DSS/STScI), GALEX, the ROSAT All-Sky Survey, and the Fermi Large Area Telescope (Fermi-LAT). Pioneer 10/11 IPP measurements provided a calibrated reference for the visible-light portion of the relative intensity comparison.
